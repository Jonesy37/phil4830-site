The United States federal government has said that “The United States is in a race to achieve global dominance in artificial intelligence.”[^1] They have described their strategy as explicitly accelerationist, prioritizing speed of innovation over safety. This strategy is due to their continued assertion that winning the race against Chinese AI developers is the foremost priority for our AI strategy. There are legitimate reasons to worry about an opponent’s adversarial AI systems; offensive cybersecurity threats are very real, and agentic coding and exploit agents accelerate this problem dramatically. However, every advance in capability presents increasing opportunities for another rogue AI incident,[^2]as well as giving anyone an opportunity to reverse engineer our own developments.[^3] We have already seen Chinese models replicate proprietary model capability at a fraction of the cost soon after those capabilities are achieved.[^4] The federal government’s strategy benefits our defensive security but poses massive detriment to the environment, society, and our world’s safety.

Diplomacy is currently focused on preventing exports to countries we are competing with and responding to incidents themselves, not preventing them. This is nowhere near effective enough; AI experts have warned countless times at the inherent risk developing a model at all has unless heavily safeguarding, which is not a primary focus of the government as of right now. Jan Leike, the former Superalignment Co-Lead at OpenAI, has said, “Over the past few years, safety culture and processes have taken a backseat to shiny products... Building smarter-than-human machines is an inherently dangerous endeavor.”

Addressing the current problems with chatbots, coding agents, and other current implementations of AI, as well as the massive potential future risks requires a coordinated international push for regulation and cooperation between nations on this issue. The most comprehensive framework we have seen addressing AI has been from the EU with their “Artificial Intelligence Act.” However, this is also vastly insufficient. This needs to be an effort that includes the countries that host the most capable models, namely China and the USA, as well as everyone who has a stake in the world's future, which is presumably every nation on Earth.

We have seen these efforts before; the response to nuclear power was an unprecedented exercise in global cooperation and resolve, addressing one of the most prominent threats of the 20th century. The efforts are not without issue, and still don’t function perfectly today, but continue to meaningfully contain the threat of nuclear weapons. The CEO of Google Deepmind, Demis Hassabis, has proposed a system of governance similar but much more advanced to that of climate change, saying, “I think we have to start with something like the IPCC, where it’s a scientific and research agreement with reports, and then build up from there… Then what I’d like to see eventually is an equivalent of a Cern for AI safety that does research into that – but internationally. And then maybe there’s some kind of equivalent one day of the IAEA, which actually audits these things.” This would require a major  global shift towards more robust regulatory systems, ones that place scientific consensus at the forefront of policy-making.

We can already see this shift gaining support. In August of this year over 1,000 AI frontier employees asked that: “the U.S. government support an international effort to develop the technical and governance tools needed to deliberately pace the frontier of automated AI development.” (pacingthefrontier, 2026) There have already been numerous nation-specific AI related advisory institutes that have been created in the past few years, along with many independent organizations, like the FutureOfLife institute.
