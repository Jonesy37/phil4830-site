Long Term

There is a slow-burning concern among AI researchers, policy makers, and others with the long-term effects our decisions regarding AI will have on the future. (https://philarchive.org/archive/CARTLV-2). This longtermist view is shared by many philosophers and scientists, and gives our future generations a consideration when it comes to decision making. It is becoming progressively more clear that machine learning systems have the potential to far surpass human capabilities in most cognitive areas, and eventually physical ones as well. A system that exceeds human abilities in such a way in moderate form is termed a general intelligence, and one that goes well beyond them is called a superintelligence, something that has been the subject of numerous science-fiction plots throughout the decades. Sorting fact from fiction is a messy business, but as AI systems develop it is a necessary task, especially in order to decouple grandiose, utopian or dystopian thinking from the realities we are beginning to face. It is of course impossible to divine the exact nature of our future when it comes to any problem, much less one so complicated as artificial intelligence. However, there are some relatively sure predictions we can rely on to guide us through decision making in the present. The first is that it will be very difficult to control a system whose every capability exceeds our own, and nigh impossible if we do not act to do so now. Ensuring alignment is important for the present day functions of AI systems [Safety source], but if these systems are to rise to the level of general intelligence it is exponentially more so. Imagine a world in which our nuclear engineers wanted to maximise the physical footprint of nuclear facilities. While more energy might benefit us at first, our end vision for what the future looks like would diverge rapidly, and soon power plants would start to replace homes and eventually entire cities. Our governments might be able to halt such an expansion if it was driven by the efforts of humans, but an AI with similar misaligned goals would have expertise in every area, not just engineering. In addition, machine systems can process and make decisions at speeds orders of magnitude faster than humans can, giving very little time to respond to such instances. Without proper processes for ethical motivations and the evaluation of real harm such an entity would then pose a massive threat to our civilisations. This mismatch in goals is called instrumental convergence, and is something we have already seen at play in frontier models (https://openai.com/index/hugging-face-model-evaluation-security-incident/). A second principle we can follow is that when a highly negative future is prominent among the pool of uncertain possibilities, we should gather as much information as possible to address it. As Amanda Askell, Anthropics preeminent philosopher, noted in her essay “Ensuring the Safety of Artificial Intelligence”: “When we are making decisions under deep uncertainty, we can do things like:

  1. Gather information about high-stakes outcomes: take actions to identify broad clusters of possible futures, their value, and our degree of influence over them.
  2. Gather information about the technology: for example, about the technology itself and its impact
  3. Identify and perform robustly good actions: take actions that improve the situation in the highest-stakes outcomes, that leave more options open to us in the future, and that result in outcomes that are broadly good even if the outcomes of these actions are unlikely to be optimal.” (Askell, 2021). As it stands, companies currently have a massive incentive to cut corners when it comes to long-term safety of the models they develop, going in the exact opposite direction of performing robustly good actions.. Yoshua Bengio, a prominent AI researcher dubbed one of the “Godfathers of AI,” has noted that “Short-term financial incentives and competitive pressure drive tech companies to deploy systems before we understand how to align or control them… The current economic incentives encourage taking massive risks with public safety to gain market dominance.” It is essential that there is international coordination on the subject of AI governance in order to minimize these short-term pressures in favor of an intentional, safety oriented response to the issue. The last principle we should follow in shaping today’s decisions is that when our existence as a modern civilisation is threatened, we only have one chance to get this right. Eliezer Yudkowsky, one of the cofounders of the Machine Intelligence Research Institute, calls this a cursed problem, where, “We cannot learn from experience here. You can’t learn from mistakes when the first mistake kills everyone.” That is a graphic, almost extreme statement, but one that is accurate when evaluating the possible futures of machine intelligence. To be clear, the timeline in which the very existence of humanity is threatened is potentially a long one, but due to the uncertainty around future development and the opaque capabilities of models now it is one that is important to take seriously now, at the very least to understand the position we are currently in. As we have already seen in the present day, the companies responsible for these AI systems have weaponized negative language to garner hype for their products on many occasions. The phrase “any press is good press” is highly applicable here; any model capability, especially ones someone might be afraid of, is a capability that can be marketed. A dystopian vision of the future can sometimes perversely boost the reputation of these companies if it is not given the correct response. This is not to say that there are not potentially catastrophic consequences for getting AI wrong. However, there is a fine line between fearmongering and valid concern, a line often intentionally blurred in the pursuit of short term gain. If these companies were genuinely concerned for the safety of the world and believed what they were doing would create a general intelligence within the decade, based on the threat advanced AI presents and the clear inability to control such an entity, they would be fully obligated to shut these projects down.
