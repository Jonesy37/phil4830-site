Social

Common Sense Media surveyed 1,060 American teenagers between 13 and 17 and published the results in July 2025. 72% had used an AI companion at least once. Just over half used one a few times a month or more. 31% said their conversations with an AI were as satisfying as talking to a real friend.

The same survey found that two-thirds of teens found AI conversations less satisfying than human ones, and that 80% still spent more time with actual friends. The thirteen and fourteen year olds were more likely than the older teens to trust what an AI told them. Common Sense recommended that nobody under 18 use companion apps until stronger safeguards exist.

That 31% figure gets quoted often, and out of context. A third of teenagers finding a chatbot conversation comparable to a friendship is either alarming or unremarkable depending on what you think teenage friendship conversations are like. The survey doesn’t settle that.

The survey can’t tell us whether any of this changes anything. It’s simply a snapshot. Teens who talk to chatbots a lot might be lonelier because of it, or they might have been lonelier first and thus are more likely to talk with one.

There’s some better evidence on that. A four-week randomized controlled trial run through MIT and OpenAI found that voice-based interaction produced a small reduction in loneliness, while heavy daily use increased it, correlating with more loneliness, more dependence, and less socializing offline. A separate study of more than 1,100 people who use AI companions found that participants with fewer human relationships were more likely to seek out chatbots in the first place, and that heavy emotional disclosure to an AI signaled lower wellbeing.

This is interesting because light use of AI companions looks fine, possibly somewhat helpful. Heavy use looks bad, and the people most likely to become heavy users are the ones who already had the fewest people to talk to. Whether the technology is causing the harm or collecting the already-harmed is a question that studies can’t yet answer.

This argument has happened before, which is the main reason to be cautious about it now.

Jonathan Haidt has argued for many years that smartphones and social media caused a collapse in adolescent mental health. Candice Odgers, looking at the same literature in Nature, concluded that hundreds of researchers looking for those large effects have turned up a mixture of negligible results, small associations, and mixed findings, nearly all of it correlational, with evidence that the causal arrow sometimes runs backwards. While both of them are serious scholars, and both have read the studies, they disagree about what the studies show.

That disagreement cannot be resolved here. The reason it is brought up is that the AI companion literature is currently at roughly the stage the social media literature was at fifteen years ago. Small samples, short timeframes, mostly correlational, and a lot of media coverage.

Misinformation is another facet of this conversation.

Going into 2024, there was concern that generative AI would flood elections with convincing fakes. However, the Indian fact-checking outfit Boom Live examined 258 election-related fact-checks and found AI-generated content in twelve of them. The News Literacy Project found that “cheap fakes,” meaning ordinary video clips taken out of context, misleading captions, and selective editing, appeared about seven times more often than anything AI-generated. Researchers who reviewed the global 2024 election cycle at Harvard’s Ash Center came to similar conclusions.

The technology to make convincing fakes existed and was widely available. People mostly used older, cruder methods, because those already worked. Manipulating people doesn’t require convincing fakes, only motivated audiences.

Two years is a short window, detection methods had lots of resources during a period of intense attention, and “no measurable effect on outcomes” does not mean “no effect.”

One thing that there is not much research on but that is very curious, is substitution. Have people started asking everyday questions to a chatbot that they used to ask a person?

If you would have called your sister about a decision and instead you asked a chatbot, something happened. The call you didn’t make was also a small deposit in a relationship. There is no good longitudinal data on this, partly because it’s so recent and partly because it’s hard to instrument. This is an open question, not a finding.

Every company building these systems has a commercial interest in engagement, and companion apps in particular are built to capture attention and retention. This isn’t a conspiracy, but an observation of product design, and it should inform how any research funded by the same companies is read. Including the MIT and OpenAI trial above, which was cited anyway because it’s the best-designed study found.
