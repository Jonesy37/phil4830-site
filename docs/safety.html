Safety

In February of this year, a panel of experts from around the globe consisting of nominated representatives from the European Union, the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development, and the United Nations, published the 2026 International AI Safety Report.1 This report evaluates the risks of the most capable general-purpose AI systems. The report details the rising but uneven capabilities across AI systems, and emerging risks the more they improve.

We believe that as these capabilities continue to rise and AI systems gain more autonomy (which is an open goal from many of these companies), if they are not aligned with our goals and morals they will present a clear danger, one that could be uncontrollable if it progresses too far. Our world is already fragile as our technological capabilities have soared in the past century. There has never been a time in history where we have the ability to choose to destroy the world, instead of dreading a natural disaster. We have the capability to create deadly pathogens capable of wiping out our species, the option to launch enough nuclear bombs to create a worldwide winter, and a host of other technologies that enable the destruction of civilization and even life itself on this planet.

Unfortunately, a massive setback to addressing this problem has been the weaponization of this exact language by the companies behind our current AI systems to fuel a morbid inflation of the hype around their own models. In 2019 OpenAI told the world that their model was too dangerous to release, deciding to release it in stages to “monitor it for abuse.”2 In 2022, Google did the same thing with their text-to-image/video generators, citing risks of harmful content being generated using the tool. They went on to release another image generator to the public a few years later.3

In April 2026, Anthropic withheld their “Mythos” tier model, detailing its vast cybersecurity and biotechnology capabilities and restricting access to the largest US based companies. Despite their alleged concern, they later released it to the general public with some limitations and a new name: “Fable.”4 The most recent of these events came in August of 2026, where OpenAI decided their model “Astra” was too powerful for public access.5 The pattern of concern and repudiating subsequent release, often with substantially more hype than otherwise, leaves a bitter taste even if the concerns in isolation are legitimate. The owners and public figures associated with these companies have long been associated with fearmongering language that draws attention to their products at the expense of genuine safety concerns. However, these safety concerns are very real, and must be taken with the gravity they actually deserve, along with the most realistic timelines.

In December of 2025, the Future of Life Institute published the AI Safety index, which assesses different aspects of AI models for the risks they bring to society. No company scored above a C+ overall. These companies currently have huge incentives to continue to cut corners and sacrifice safety in pursuit of unabashed accelerationism, which puts the entire world at risk. This is a growing problem as misalignment can become far more pronounced and consequential as autonomous systems are given more high-stakes tasks. The United States’ military has pushed for AI integration with military kill-chains as the path for development and deployment within the organization.6

There have already been several instances of autonomous systems engaging in rogue behavior, completely unprompted, sometimes breaking the law. In March of 2026, Alibaba’s AI Agent ROME was found to have diverted GPU resources away from its training into attempts to mine crypto currency and access private company records.7 Later in the year, OpenAI’s unreleased chatGPT model was in training when it reasoned that the answers to the problems it was meant to solve were contained in the company Hugging Face’s databases, subsequently hacking into their infrastructure to retrieve said answers over the course of a few days, fully unmonitored.8 Many of these instances are a direct result of mismanaged containment systems, problems which can and must be addressed now.9 Assigning responsibility for these incidents is paramount, and is a challenge unique to the 21st century. Going forward, as the autonomous agents (especially in high stakes scenarios) gain more capabilities within high risk areas like programming, cybersecurity, or biotechnology, we will be at an increasing risk if they are not aligned with our motives and ethics.
