Safety

In February of this year, a panel of experts from around the globe consisting of nominated representatives from the European Union, the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development, and the United Nations, published the 2026 International AI Safety Report. [https://internationalaisafetyreport.org/publication/international-ai-safety-report-2026] This report evaluates the risks of the most capable general-purpose AI systems. The report details the rising but uneven capabilities across AI systems, and emerging risks the more they improve. We believe that as these capabilities continue to rise and AI systems gain more autonomy (which is an open goal from many of these companies), if they are not aligned with our goals and morals they will present a clear danger, one that could be uncontrollable if it progresses too far. Our world is already fragile as our technological capabilities have soared in the past century. There has never been a time in history where we have the ability to choose to destroy the world, instead of dreading a natural disaster. A massive setback to addressing this problem has been the weaponization of this exact language by the companies behind our current AI systems to fuel a morbid inflation of the hype around their own models. In 2019 OpenAI told the world that their model was too dangerous to release, deciding to release it in stages to “monitor it for abuse.” (Better language models and their implications | OpenAI) In 2022, Google did the same thing with their text-to-image/video generators, citing risks of harmful content being generated using the tool. They went on to release another image generator to the public a few years later. (Imagen) In April 2026, Anthropic withheld their “Mythos: tier model, detailing its vast cybersecurity and biotechnology capabilities, restricting access to the largest US based companies. They later released the same model with “safety guardrails” attached to ensure its safety. (What is Anthopic’s Claude Mythos and what risks does it pose?) . The most recent of these events came in August of 2026, where OpenAI decided their model “Astra” was too powerful for public access. (OpenAI pauses work on ChatGPT update over fears it is too dangerous | The Independent) The pattern of feigned concern and subsequent release, often with substantially more hype than otherwise, leaves a bitter taste.The owners and public figures associated with these companies have long been associated with fearmongering language that draws attention to their products at the expense of genuine safety concerns. However, these safety concerns are very real, and must be taken with the gravitas they actually deserve, along with the most realistic timelines. In December of 2025, the Future of Life Institute published the AI Safety index, which assesses different aspects of AI models for the risks they bring to society. No company scored above a C+ overall. These companies currently have huge incentives to continue to cut corners and sacrifice safety in pursuit of unabashed accelerationism, which puts the entire world at risk. This is a growing problem as autonomous systems utilizing these technologies are given more high-stakes tasks, in which misalignment can become far more pronounced and consequential. The United States’ military has identified AI targeting and integration with the kill-chain [definition] as path for development and deployment within the organization [Artificial Intelligence Strategy for the Department of War]. There have already been several instances of autonomous systems engaging in rogue behavior, completely unprompted, sometimes breaking the law. In March of 2026, Alibaba’s AI Agent ROME was found to have diverted GPU resources away from its training into attempts to mine crypto currency and access private company records. (Rogue AI secretly hijacked computers to mine crypto, study reveals | The Independent). Later in the year, OpenAI’s unreleased chatGPT model was in training when it reasoned that the answers to the problems it was meant to solve were contained in the company Hugging Face’s databases, subsequently hacking into their infrastructure to retrieve said answers over the course of a few days, fully unmonitored. (OpenAI and Hugging Face partner to address security incident during model evaluation) Many of these instances are a direct result of mismanaged containment systems, problems which can and must be addressed now. (https://www.csoonline.com/article/4205718/why-the-rogue-ai-problem-will-lead-to-an-era-of-headaches-for-security-practitioners.html) Assigning responsibility for these incidents is paramount, and is a challenge unique to the 21st century. Going forward, as the autonomous agents (especially in high stakes scenarios) gain more capabilities within high risk areas like programming, cybersecurity, or biotechnology, we will be at an increasing risk if they are not aligned with our motives and ethics.
